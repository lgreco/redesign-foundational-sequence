## Putting It All Together: Loops, Lists, and Strings

We continued working on a program that takes a sentence and pulls out all the words longer than a given number of letters. This brought together everything we've covered so far: a list of words, a loop that walks through that list, and an `if` check inside the loop.

## Breaking a Sentence Into Words

We used the `split` method to break a sentence into a list of words, cutting at the spaces by default. We confirmed this by counting: a 15-word sentence gave us a list with 15 items, each one reachable by its position, starting at position 0.

## Counting and Looping

We used `len()` to find out how many words are in the list, instead of hard-coding that number — so the program keeps working no matter how long the sentence is. We saw that `len()` does double duty: it tells us how many words are in the list, and inside the loop, it also tells us how many letters are in each individual word.

## Why a String Is Really a List of Characters

A student asked why one part of the code uses brackets with a position number and another part doesn't. This led to a deeper point: under the hood, a string like "hello" is stored as a list of single characters, even though it looks and behaves like one solid piece of text to us. That's why the same `len()` works on both lists and strings.

## Two Ways to Loop

We compared two styles of loop: the traditional version, which counts through positions one at a time, and a simpler version that goes "for every word in this list" without tracking a position number. The simpler version is easier to read for going through everything in order, but the traditional version is still needed for trickier jobs, like grabbing only every other word.
