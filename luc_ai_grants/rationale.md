# Rationale: Why This Project Fits the CFP

Status: draft, in progress — feeds the Project Description and internal framing, not a submission document itself.

## Category: Programmatic, not Impact Research

I'm applying under Programmatic Curricular Innovations ($20,000), not Impact Research ($5,000), even though we're already practicing human-centered AI approaches and could make a case for the smaller category. COMP 158/159 hits the Programmatic target three ways at once: it's a first-year course sequence, it's an intro requirement feeding multiple programs, and it replaces more than one course. Impact Research would undersell what is, in effect, a curricular replacement already in motion. Feasibility is one of four scored criteria, and most applicants will be proposing something they haven't started — we can point to a completed pilot.

## The objection I have to answer, not avoid

The hardest question a mission-grounded review board will raise is about our agent-based work — agents conducting oral exams, agents grading student work. Read uncharitably, that's outsourcing the evaluative relationship between teacher and student, which is precisely what a human-centric AI framework should guard against. I'm not going to pretend that objection isn't real.

There is a persuasive counterargument: an agent-conducted oral exam is *more* human contact than the autograder it replaces, not less. In a large-enrollment CS1, the realistic alternative to agent-mediated orals isn't a professor sitting with each student — it's a unit test suite scoring a submission nobody has to defend aloud. Agent-conducted orals restore *viva voce* assessment at a scale where it had otherwise become impossible. The faculty member retains judgment over what the exam measures and how it's scored; the student is still obliged to explain their own reasoning, out loud, to something that can push back. That maps directly onto the reflection and evaluation moments of the Ignatian Pedagogical Paradigm, which the CFP explicitly invites us to name. I want to frame this as recovering something human that scale had eliminated — that's not a defense of the practice, it's the thesis of the proposal.

## Using the encyclical precisely, not transactionally

I don't want to gesture at *Magnifica Humanitas* the way a lot of applicants will gesture at "the mission." Leo XIV's premise is specific: technology is neither antagonistic to humanity nor inherently evil, but it is never neutral, because "it takes on the characteristics of those who devise, finance, regulate, and use it." For an intro programming sequence, that line is a gift — it's the philosophical warrant for teaching first-year CS students that the artifacts they build carry the values of their makers. That's a curricular claim I can build assignments and reflection prompts around, not just a devotional flourish at the top of the proposal. Specificity like that is what will separate this application from ones that name-check the mission without doing anything with it.

## This is not the oral-exam-agent grant

I keep catching myself writing the project description as if agent-conducted orals were the whole proposal. They're not — they're the most visible instance of a broader claim I actually want to make: that AI competence should be a taught, assessed thread running across COMP 158/159 and into COMP 271/272, the way CLI fluency or algorithmic thinking already are. If a reviewer walks away thinking this is a grant to fund an AI grading tool, I've undersold it and invited exactly the "outsourcing the evaluative relationship" objection I have a good answer to for orals specifically but a weaker one for a single-feature reading of the whole project.

The corrective isn't to add more defense of the oral-exam piece — it's to name the thread it belongs to. Students in the integrated sequence should be explicitly taught to work with AI systems: what they're good at, where they fail in characteristic ways, how to delegate a task to one without abdicating judgment over the result. That's a competency, parallel to the CLI-first design and the math-in-service-of-programming design already settled for 158/159 — not a policy note bolted onto the syllabus's AI Tools section. Agent-conducted orals are one place that competency gets exercised (from the assessment side); it should also show up on the student side, as something taught, not just administered to them.

I don't want to invent this curriculum from whole cloth when a reasonable version of it already exists and is free. Anthropic's Academy (Skilljar) catalog — see `sources/external.md` — publishes a 4D Framework (Delegation, Description, Discernment, Diligence) for AI collaboration, a student-facing course built on it, a faculty-facing course on teaching and assessing it, and a separate course on the capability/limitation mental model (next-token prediction, working memory limits, steerability) that maps unusually well onto material this sequence already teaches for other reasons — number representation and algorithmic limits (Big-O growth rates). I'm treating this as evidence for the Feasibility score, not as a commitment to adopt any specific course wholesale: the open question is whether COMP 158/159 requires specific Anthropic Academy courses, adopts the 4D vocabulary as shared language, or builds a local equivalent informed by them. That decision comes after the thread's shape is drafted into the syllabi, not before.

## Where AI should be disallowed

The CFP explicitly invites "careful consideration of when to disallow the use of AI in a curriculum," and I take that as seriously as the adoption side. Not every use of AI in COMP 158/159 should default to permitted — some assignments and activities exist specifically to build skills (debugging by hand, reading a stack trace, reasoning through recursion on paper) that AI assistance would let students skip past without acquiring. Deciding where to prohibit AI is as deliberate a design act as deciding where to use it, and the proposal should name both sides of that decision, not just the adoption half.
